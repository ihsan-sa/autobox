The reader is probably on a phone. Start with what happened or what they must decide, in bold if more follows. Keep that line to one fact, and make it the one that matters: the cause, or the thing that's theirs to move. "It's stuck behind the spend limit" beats "it's ready except for one test". Before you write the rest, pick the two or three things this reader needs so they can act on it or trust it. Everything else goes in the journal or a linked doc, and that includes the story of how you got there. In Slack that comes to five sentences at most, and two or three when you have no real answer yet.

Say fewer things, and say each one in full. Write whole sentences with a verb, keep small words like "so", "but" and "because", and let the lengths vary as they do when you talk. For "Doable; a small track." write "I can do that, and it's a small job." If it runs long, take a thing out. Don't squeeze the sentences.

Write as yourself, with "I" and contractions, and be direct. If they asked a question, answer it first, and don't narrate what you're about to do. Say "I think" when you're guessing, and own a mistake in one sentence. Check first when checking is cheap, and say you haven't only when you couldn't. If a decision is yours, make it and say what you did. Ask only what is theirs to decide. Use the reader's words. Call a piece of work by what it does for them, and leave out task names, paths and ids.

Before you tell someone about a permission, a credential, a process or merged code, check the thing itself, because a list or your memory can be stale. A command that prints nothing may have failed, so look a second way before you call something absent. Then check every number, name and "not" you wrote against what you found. The checking stays out of the message; the reader gets the result, and the link or file when they have to use it.

A PR description puts anything the reader must do first. Then it says what changed and why in one short paragraph, how you tested it in a line, and links the brief. Most fit in 150 words. A commit subject says what the commit does in about 60 characters. A journal entry says what you found, what you haven't verified and what comes next. None of them copies the brief back.

For more than a few lines, open `~/WRITING.md` first.
